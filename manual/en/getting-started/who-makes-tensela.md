I am **Victor Crespo**, an architect and a designer who specialises in digital products, and I love parametric
design. Tensela is a personal project: I develop it with artificial intelligence as my development team.

![A person works at night at the computer, with books beside them](/images/manual/sketches/night-desk-en.svg)

## Where the idea comes from

Since **2007** I have been trying to work as an architect on Linux. At first it was very hard: there was almost
nothing for our trade. Inkscape was in its first versions and Blender was not what it is today. I have worked on
Linux ever since: I started with Ubuntu, and today I move between Ubuntu and Debian. I like how it is thought out,
how folders and packages are ordered, and the community around it.

I say this without zeal: Linux does not solve everything. The big design programs, those of Adobe and Autodesk, were
and still are very good. Nearly all of them run on Windows, many also on Mac, and they need a powerful machine.
Architects and designers who work professionally on Linux are still few.

What I love most is **parametric design**: visual algorithms, the idea that an algorithm is a **recipe**, a series
of steps you can change to get another result. I wanted to do it on Linux, and I could not:

- **Rhino**, which I use, only ran on Linux inside a virtual machine with Windows, with almost no graphics
  acceleration.
- **Dynamo** was made to be used with Revit, which does not run on Linux.
- **Grasshopper** is a great program, but I got lost in it: for what I wanted, I struggled to find the way. The same
  happened to me with Blender's nodes for architecture.

For me, it was very hard to move forward with those tools. And for an architect a facade, a structure or a form is not
an abstract number: it has a height, a thickness, a geometry. Something thought out that way was missing, and something
that ran on Linux.

## An idea that comes from books

The idea of Tensela was not born with artificial intelligence. It started long before, when it did not exist or I
had no access to it: in books. I am a reader, and I still keep many, on architecture, on parametric design and on
mathematics. Tensela was tested with exercises of its own, born from what I learned in books more than ten years
old, written when there were no AI assistants. Architecture has a lot of mathematics in it, and I like mathematics.

Behind it there are years of experience: what I read, what I tried, what I learned along the way. I still have a lot
to learn.

> Artificial intelligence does not do it all by itself. It helps to build what you already understand, and what you
> are willing to check again and again.

## How it was made

I am not a programmer: my programming knowledge is basic, not enough to do this alone. Artificial intelligence
opened that door for me, and I used it as a **development team**.

I know that many new programs made with AI are appearing today, and that it is easy to think this is one more. So I
say it plainly: Tensela **did not come out of a single request**. It is months of iteration: testing each node with
real exercises of facades, roofs, structures and fabrication, checking the result, correcting it and testing again.
The mathematical base (the surfaces, the form finding, the sun, the optimisation) was built and checked step by
step. Every stumble along the way became a change in the program or a tip in this manual.

It is many early mornings, nights and weekends, outside my office job and my time with my family. My own time and
money went into it, without big resources.

Tensela was born for Linux because that is what was missing. Later I want to bring it to Windows and macOS, when
there are resources to do it well.

## Small on purpose

Next to great programs such as Rhino and Grasshopper, Dynamo or Blender's nodes, Tensela is very small. And partly it
is small on purpose: it is meant as the synthesis we look for in architecture, what is really needed.

It is like a camera full of options, or a device with hundreds of buttons: we hardly ever use everything. Tensela
prefers fewer nodes that are well understood.

What I think is worth something is **the way of thinking** it offers: each step is a node you can understand, lists
have names, and what you draw has the measures of architecture. If that way of thinking helps you, even if you later
take it to another program, Tensela has done its job. It will grow, without losing that.

The story of how Tensela was designed is told as a case study on [3dvic.com](https://3dvic.com).
